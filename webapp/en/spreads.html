Short instructions

How to use the atlas

  1. Name one task.Not “what will happen?”, but “what will help promote sales in the next three months?”
  2. Choose the smallest useful spread.Three to five cards are often clearer than ten. Use a larger spread only when the question genuinely involves many factors.
  3. Define the positions in advance.Do not change a position's meaning after an uncomfortable card appears.
  4. Read the connections.Axes, pairs and sequences first, then assemble the cards into one story.
  5. Check with reality.Separate symbolic speculation from verifiable facts.
Clarifying card

Draw one only for a narrow question, such as “What exactly does this card mean in the obstacle position?” or “What does it definitely not mean here?” Do not keep drawing until you obtain the answer you want.

Sources and editorial decisions

Classic · 1911

A. E. Waite — Celtic Cross

The primary source is retained for the spread's history, while the catalog uses the more familiar modern position labels: the core, obstacle, foundation, past, goal, near future, and right-hand column.

Classic · 1912

Papus — the Etteilla method

The text confirms working rows of 26, 17, and 11 cards and explicitly describes the remaining 24 cards as a discarded packet. That differs from the version presented in the supplied source file.

History · museum

Victoria and Albert Museum

The divinatory use of the Tarot dates back to the late 18th century; claims of ancient Egyptian origin have not been historically verified.

Modern editorial version

“Egyptian Divination” (Etteilla method)

This version retains the 78-card division algorithm, the paired reading of rows from the edges toward the center, and an alternative reading of the fourth group as “Fate.” The disagreement with Papus is stated openly.
