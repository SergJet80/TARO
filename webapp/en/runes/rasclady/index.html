3. Modern Methods of Runic Diagnostics

This section describes modern esoteric practice, not a historically attested system of the ancient Germanic peoples. A diagnostic cast does not prove that a supernatural influence exists; it is more useful as a symbolic way to formulate hypotheses and then check ordinary causes of what is happening.

One rune

Question: ‘What is the principal nature of the influence on this situation?’ One rune can show a general tone, but it provides too little information for a conclusion that an influence is or is not present. A difficult rune may describe an ordinary crisis, conflict, lack of resources, or delay.

Three runes

  1. The current state of the situation.
  2. The principal source of strain or support.
  3. What will help change the situation.

Extended seven-rune diagnostic cast

  1. Overall state.
  2. What the person perceives as the problem.
  3. A possible external factor.
  4. A possible internal factor.
  5. The area affected most strongly.
  6. What sustains the problem.
  7. A practical step toward changing the situation.

Rule of interpretation

If a cast looks ‘negative,’ formulate at least two explanations first: an esoteric one and an everyday or psychological one. Then check the facts: actual conflicts, money, documents, communication, deadlines, the participants’ actions, and other observable causes.

5. Magical Functions by Category

The list below is a modern practical classification, not an ancient canon.

  • Resources and material foundation: Fehu, Jera, Ingwaz, Othala.
  • Will and victory: Uruz, Tiwaz, Sowilo, Thurisaz.
  • Protection and boundaries: Algiz, Eihwaz, Othala, Thurisaz.
  • Movement and roads: Raidho, Ehwaz, Laguz.
  • Communication and knowledge: Ansuz, Kenaz, Mannaz.
  • Partnership and relationships: Gebo, Wunjo, Ehwaz, Laguz, Berkano.
  • Growth and creation: Berkano, Jera, Ingwaz, Fehu.
  • Stopping and restriction: Isa, Nauthiz.
  • Destruction and radical restructuring: Hagalaz, Thurisaz.
  • Mystery, probability, and searching for what is hidden: Perthro, Laguz, Kenaz.
  • Transition and transformation: Eihwaz, Dagaz, Hagalaz, Ingwaz.

6. Sources and Methodology

Two layers of sources were used when expanding this material.

Historical and academic layer

  1. Bruce Dickins. Runic and Heroic Poems of the Old Teutonic Peoples. Cambridge University Press, 1915 — translations of the Anglo-Saxon, Norwegian, and Icelandic rune poems.
  2. Tom Birkett. Runes: A Concise History. Oxford University Press, 2025 — a modern academic history of runic writing and its later reception.
  3. Mindy MacLeod, Bernard Mees. Runic Amulets and Magic Objects. Boydell & Brewer, 2006 — a study of archaeological runic amulets and magical inscriptions.
  4. Sigrdrífumál (the Lay of Sigrdrifa) — literary evidence for categories of ‘victory,’ ‘ale,’ ‘speech,’ and other runes; it is not used as a table of meanings for all 24 signs.
  5. E. A. Melnikova. Scandinavian Runic Inscriptions: New Finds and Interpretations and other studies of Scandinavian epigraphy — for the general historical context of inscriptions.

Modern esoteric layer

Modern divinatory and magical meanings were compared with the tradition formed by twentieth- and twenty-first-century authors and practitioners, including Edred Thorsson, Freya Aswynn, and Diana L. Paxson. The text does not present these meanings as an attested ancient Germanic canon.

Editorial principle

Historical meanings rely primarily on rune names, rune poems, and epigraphy. Divinatory meanings, reversed positions, staves, diagnostic layouts, and most combinations are identified as modern practice. Disputed reconstructions—especially Uruz, Kenaz, Perthro, and Algiz—are not presented as uncontested fact.

7. Conclusion

The Elder Futhark can be studied from several perspectives at once: as a historical writing system, as a set of symbolic images preserved in the rune poems, and as the foundation of a modern divinatory and magical tradition. These levels are connected, but they are not identical.

The practical value of this distinction is that readers can use a modern reference without mistaking later interpretations for literal knowledge from the age of the ancient Germanic peoples. Runes remain a rich system of symbols when historical caution does not obstruct modern practice and modern practice does not rewrite history.

The practices described are modern esoteric and reference material, not a historically attested system of the ancient Germanic peoples. The material is for reference and entertainment.
