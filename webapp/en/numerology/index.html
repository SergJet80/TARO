NUMEROLOGY AND TAROT SUITS

A systematic approach to reading the numbered Minor Arcana

From Ace to Ten: the number gives the stage of a situation’s development; the suit gives the area in which that stage unfolds

An educational article for a Tarot reference guide

Basis: the logic of a lecture on the systematic meanings of the numbered Arcana; missing passages have been reconstructed and checked against the Rider–Waite–Smith tradition and contemporary teaching sources.

The key formula

CARD MEANING = NUMBER × SUIT × CONTEXT OF THE SCENE

The formula is not mathematical multiplication — it is a way to combine two layers of meaning.

The formula is not mathematical multiplication. It is a way to combine two layers of meaning.
The number answers the question: ‘What is happening to the situation as a process?’ It begins, chooses a direction, develops, consolidates, encounters a crisis, recovers, faces a test, enters a predictable process, reaches an outcome, or comes to a close.
The suit answers the question: ‘What exactly is this happening to?’ Will and action, thoughts and decisions, feelings and relationships, or material resources.
The card’s scene clarifies the particular nuance taken on by the combination of number and suit. This is why the same number can look favorable in one suit and difficult in another.
This idea is consistent with A. E. Waite himself: in describing the Minor Arcana, he emphasized that their symbolism depends on the particular significance of a number combined with a particular suit. Contemporary systematic Tarot schools also use numerical patterns to help people understand and remember the cards.

Why use numerology for the Minor Arcana?

A beginning Tarot reader faces an understandable problem: the forty numbered Minor Arcana seem like forty separate meanings to memorize. Yet they can be read as a system. This article takes precisely that approach: the number shows the stage the situation has reached, while the suit shows the area of life in which that stage unfolds.

This method does not discard the classic card meanings or replace the image. It explains why a card has its particular meaning. Logic takes the place of rote memory: once the ten numerical principles and four suits are understood, most interpretations can be reconstructed even without a reference guide.

Within the lecture’s framework, Wands and Swords are considered more ‘masculine’ or active suits: their energy calls for initiative, action, pressure, and decisions. Cups and Pentacles are more ‘feminine’ or receptive: the ability to receive, hold, become filled, and preserve matters here. This is especially vivid in the Aces: a hand grips the Wand and Sword, while the Cup and Pentacle seem to be offered on an open palm.

Note. These categories belong to the symbolic language of an esoteric tradition, rather than biological sex. References to health in the suit of Pentacles concern the bodily/resource layer of the card’s symbolism and do not replace medical assessment.

1. The Ace — the suit’s pure impulse

An Ace is a concentrated possibility, rather than a finished result. It is the suit’s energy before it has taken a stable form. The lecture describes Aces as an impetus, a ‘burst’ of energy, the moment when something becomes possible and calls for a path forward.

Ace of Wands
‘I want.’ Will, enthusiasm, sexual and creative energy, a strong desire to begin. This impulse calls for action: the idea has not yet been realized, but there is already plenty of fuel.
Ace of Swords
‘I know.’ Insight, a clear thought, a decision, setting a goal, an intellectual breakthrough. The Sword cuts away what came before and sets a new direction; the card can therefore indicate firm certainty or an ultimatum.
Ace of Cups
‘I feel.’ Emotions overflow the vessel: joy, inspiration, falling in love, uplift, openness to experience. This is a powerful beginning of an emotional flow, rather than a guarantee of a lasting relationship.
Ace of Pentacles
‘I am given a resource.’ The possibility of prosperity, a practical opening, money, work, the body and health as a reserve of strength, a useful acquisition. It is essential to distinguish a gift from a completed achievement: the card shows a path toward an arch and a mountain — the resource has been received, but the summit still lies ahead.
Memory aid: the Ace gives raw potential; the suit shows exactly what can be put to use

2. The Two — duality, choice, and the first step toward realization

After the pure impulse, a second pole appears. Now ‘I want’ must be related to reality, oneself to another person, and one option to another. Twos therefore bring pairing, choice, balance, planning, and sometimes a pause: movement has not yet gathered strength.

Two of Wands
Will begins to look ahead. A person already has some power at their disposal but is assessing horizons, comparing options, and making plans. Intention expands without a full start.
Two of Swords
The mind holds two opposing decisions. Conflict is temporarily frozen, emotions are under control, and the final choice is postponed. This is managed equilibrium rather than peace as deep agreement: ‘a bad peace is better than a good war.’
Two of Cups
The Ace’s emotional energy meets another person. Reciprocity, affection, union, agreement, and attraction arise. The Two appears here literally as a pair: feelings are returned and begin to form a connection.
Two of Pentacles
Two material flows must be kept going at once. Hence the balancing of budgets, tasks, resources, and responsibilities, and the need to adapt to changing circumstances. This is not yet stability, but the art of dropping neither pentacle.
Memory aid: the Two places two poles side by side; they must be joined, compared, chosen between, or kept in balance

3. The Three — manifestation, development, and the first visible result

The Three brings the process out of internal preparation and into the world. While the situation weighed options and chose a direction in the Two, the chosen course begins to manifest in the Three. This is the number of growth, development, interaction, and the first result. The nature of that result, however, depends on the suit.

Three of Wands
The plans of the Two of Wands begin to expand in the real world. This is enterprise, trade, development, moving beyond former boundaries, and waiting for a return from a process already under way. The figure looks at ships: action has been taken, and the prospect of growth now appears.
Three of Swords
A conflict in thought ceases to be abstract and becomes lived reality. Separation, an unwelcome truth, a breakup, the pain of realization — a thought ‘pierces the heart.’ In this systematic framework, a mental contradiction becomes an actual event.
Three of Cups
Feeling extends beyond a couple and becomes a shared emotional space: friendship, celebration, support, and joy among people. This is an emotion that can be shared.
Three of Pentacles
A resource becomes skill and concrete work. Professional cooperation, learning, building a result together, and recognition of quality appear. Material energy becomes visible work.
Memory aid: the Three makes the internal external; the chosen direction begins to manifest

4. The Four — stability, form, and consolidation

The Four builds form. After the Three’s growth comes a need to consolidate the result, establish boundaries, and preserve what has been achieved. Fours therefore bring stability, protection, and order — but this is also where the risk of becoming stuck, shutting down, or holding too tightly to what exists appears.

Four of Wands
Energy gains a stable base: a home, territory, a completed stage, celebrating a result, a sense that ‘we have established ourselves here.’ This is the lightest expression of the Four’s stability.
Four of Swords
The mind stops. After tension, a pause, silence, retreat, recovery, and reflection are needed. Stability in Swords looks like the end of active conflict and mental stillness.
Four of Cups
The emotional sphere becomes so fixed that it no longer responds to anything new. Satiety, boredom, withdrawal, dissatisfaction: a person is already enclosed within a familiar emotional state and does not accept the new cup being offered.
Four of Pentacles
Material stability becomes holding on. Savings, control, ownership, security, a wish to preserve what has been achieved. At its best, sensible protection of resources; in its shadow, fear of loss and excessive rigidity.
Memory aid: the Four consolidates; at its best, support; at its worst, stagnation and excessive fixation

5. The Five — crisis and the breakdown of the previous balance

The Five stands at the midpoint of the ten-stage cycle and breaks the Four’s stability. Its task is to move the situation out of its comfort zone. The crisis is experienced differently in each suit: through struggle, defeat, grief, or a shortage of resources.

Five of Wands
The crisis of energy appears as competition and chaotic struggle. Several wills collide: an argument, a contest, conflicting interests, a need to prove one’s right to a place.
Five of Swords
The crisis of thought and conflict becomes severe: defeat, broken relationships, humiliation, victory at too high a price, loss of respect. In the lecture’s framework, this is one of the hardest Fives because the number of crisis meets the suit most associated with conflict.
Five of Cups
The crisis of feeling is experienced as loss and disappointment. Attention is captured by the overturned cups, so the remaining emotional resource temporarily goes unnoticed. The card concerns not only loss but also the narrowing of perception through grief.
Five of Pentacles
A material crisis means a shortage: of money, health as a resource, support, stability, warmth, time, or other practical means. No scale is specified: the essence of the card is the sense of lack and the loss of former security.
Memory aid: the Five breaks the previous stability and demands change

6. The Six — recovery, exchange, and a way out of crisis

The Six follows the Five’s disruption and seeks to put the system back together. The original lecture emphasizes interaction between people: Sixes rarely exist in complete isolation. This is the number of help, coordination, exchange, and more harmonious movement.

Six of Wands
After the Five’s competition, a winner and recognition emerge. Energy comes together in one direction, efforts receive a public response, and a person sees their strength affirmed.
Six of Swords
The mind seeks a way out of conflict rather than victory within it. Diplomacy, transition, leaving acute confrontation, help with a crossing, a journey. This is the ability to stop cutting and move into calmer territory.
Six of Cups
Emotions recover through warmth, memory, familiar feelings, care, and exchange. Hence nostalgia, goodwill, support, and a return to what replenishes emotional resources.
Six of Pentacles
The Five’s material crisis gives way to the distribution of resources. Help, a gift, payment, support, charity, the balance of ‘give and receive.’ The scales are a reminder that resources are distributed within limits and conditions, rather than without end.
Memory aid: the Six rebuilds the system after a crisis through help, exchange, and coordination

7. The Seven — a test, an individual path, and one’s own response

The Six offered relative equilibrium, but the Seven disrupts it again. Now a problem does more than simply occur — a person must show how well they can act independently. The lecture draws a parallel with the Chariot: one’s own path, one’s own task, the need to maintain direction.

Seven of Wands
A test of will: one must defend what has been won, stand firm, and withstand pressure. The figure holds an advantageous position, but must still defend it.
Seven of Swords
A test of the mind: the direct route may be blocked, bringing ingenuity, an indirect maneuver, strategy, and cunning into play. The solution may look questionable to others; the key to the systematic interpretation is the attempt to resolve a problem in an unconventional way.
Seven of Cups
A test of feeling: there are too many options and desires. Fantasies, temptations, emotional confusion, and difficulty distinguishing a real desire from an appealing image.
Seven of Pentacles
A test of patience: work has been invested, but the desired material result has not yet ripened. The resource is not lost — it is still growing. The task is to avoid destroying the result through impatience.
Memory aid: the Seven tests personal strategy — exactly how a person will handle the task

8. The Eight — the law of consequences, balance, and moving with the flow

In the author’s framework, the Eight contrasts with the Seven. In the Seven, a person actively resisted and sought their own way through a test; in the Eight, much is already determined by the consequences of previous actions. The task is to see the pattern, accept the situation’s constraints, and act accordingly. ‘Karma’ is more usefully understood here as the principle of cause and effect than as a mystical verdict.

Eight of Wands
Energy has already gathered speed. Events move quickly, creating a flow of messages, journeys, actions, and opportunities. The key is to respond in time and avoid trying to slow down what is already in motion.
Eight of Swords
The consequences of mental attitudes become restrictions. A person is bound by ideas, fear, lack of information, or external constraints. One significant nuance: part of the prison may be sustained by their own belief that there is no way out.
Eight of Cups
The emotional cycle calls for departure. A person willingly leaves behind what once brought satisfaction because continuing no longer fits their inner state. This is a voluntary renunciation and a move onward.
Eight of Pentacles
The material rule is simple: results are built through repeated work. Craft, discipline, practice, honing a skill, working until it becomes automatic. There is no need to check the result every minute — the process needs to be carried out correctly.
Memory aid: the Eight shows a predictable process and its consequences — less struggle, more appropriate movement within established conditions

9. The Nine — the suit’s culmination and a personal outcome

The Nine is the final step before completion. The suit’s energy has matured, and a person is largely left face to face with what they have accumulated. The original lecture draws a parallel with the Hermit: the illustrations of the Nines show a theme of separateness, autonomy, or an inward experience of the result.

Nine of Wands
The culmination of experience gained through action. A person knows that an attack or difficulty is possible and prepares their defenses in advance. Experience becomes protection, but may also bring heightened vigilance.
Nine of Swords
The mental suit culminates in an overloaded mind: anxiety, fear, thoughts at night, despair, repeatedly going over a problem. Here the mind has reached the limit of its own painful activity.
Nine of Cups
The culmination of feelings is satisfaction, pleasure, emotional fullness, a fulfilled wish. The figure sits before their cups as though before a store of pleasure already gathered. The shadow is complacency or retreating into personal comfort.
Nine of Pentacles
The material suit culminates in independence, prosperity, comfort, and the ability to enjoy the fruits of one’s work. This resource already belongs to the person and frees them from dependence on immediate help from others.
Memory aid: the Nine is the suit’s nearly completed outcome, already experienced as one’s personal result

10. The Ten — completion, fullness, and the limit of the suit’s energy

The Ten brings what the Ace began to its logical conclusion. It is therefore both a result and the threshold of a new cycle. In favorable suits, fullness looks like complete happiness or a lasting legacy; in tense suits, it looks like overload or final destruction. The meaning is the same: developing the situation further in its previous form is almost impossible.

Ten of Wands
There is too much energy and too many tasks. The person has indeed gained much and carries the result of their own efforts, but success becomes a burden of responsibilities. This is the limit of fire: ‘I can’ becomes ‘I carry everything myself.’
Ten of Swords
Conflict and mental tension reach a point beyond which the old scenario cannot continue. A painful ending, the collapse of an idea, final defeat, or the recognition that a stage is over. The card’s paradox is that hitting bottom also puts an end to further falling.
Ten of Cups
Emotional energy reaches fullness: harmony, love, a sense of belonging within a family or group, satisfaction in relationships. This is no longer the Nine’s personal pleasure but an emotional system that includes other people.
Ten of Pentacles
A material result becomes a lasting system: home, family, property, legacy, capital, tradition, long-term security. The resource extends beyond one person and can be passed on.
Memory aid: the Ten takes the suit to its limit and closes the cycle, preparing the transition to the next one

A practical method: meaning without rote memorization

  1. Name the suit. Identify the area: action/will, mind/decision, feelings/relationships, or material resources.
  2. Name the number. Identify the stage of the process within the ten-stage cycle.
  3. Combine the two layers in one phrase. For example: ‘5 + Pentacles = a crisis of material resources’; ‘4 + Cups = emotional fixation’; ‘9 + Swords = the culmination of mental tension.’
  4. Look at the scene. The image specifies the form: what the figure is doing, the direction of movement, whether other people are present, and what is being held or lost.
  5. Return the card to the question. The same card reads differently in questions about work, relationships, plans, one’s inner state, or timing.
  6. Only then add the nuances of a particular school. Classic keywords, reversed positions, neighboring cards, elements, and other systems should refine the basic structure rather than replace it.

Examples of systematic interpretation

Five of Pentacles
5 = crisis; Pentacles = a resource. This gives: a crisis of resources → a lack of money, strength, health as a physical resource, support, or stability.
Three of Cups
3 = manifestation and development; Cups = feelings. This gives: feelings extend into a wider circle → shared joy, friendship, celebration, emotional support.
Four of Swords
4 = consolidation and a pause; Swords = mind and conflict. This gives: mental/conflict activity stops → a pause, retreat, rest, recovery.
Seven of Pentacles
7 = a test; Pentacles = a practical result. This gives: a test of the material process → the result has not yet ripened; patience and an assessment of the work invested are needed.
Ten of Wands
10 = a limit and completion; Wands = action and workload. This gives: action and responsibility have accumulated to their limit → success becomes a heavy burden.

Why the same number produces different cards

The Four stabilizes. In Wands, this may be a celebration of consolidated success; in Swords, a pause and rest; in Cups, emotional stagnation; in Pentacles, holding on to property.
The Nine brings culmination. In Cups, this is satisfaction; in Pentacles, independence; in Wands, accumulated defensive experience; in Swords, the maximum strain of anxious thought.
The Ten completes. In Cups, fullness is experienced as happiness; in Pentacles, as a lasting legacy; in Wands, as being overloaded by what has been achieved; in Swords, as the definitive end of a destructive scenario.

Connections between numbers and the Major Arcana

A useful association rather than a strict rule: the Major Arcanum is a general archetype; the numbered card is its everyday expression within a suit.

III The Empress helps us remember the Three as growth, fruitfulness, and a result becoming visible.
IV The Emperor supports the idea of the Four as structure, boundaries, and stability.
VI The Lovers recalls the theme of joining and alignment that is especially apparent in the Sixes.
VII The Chariot represents moving along one’s own path, the need to hold a position, and meeting a test; this association is explicitly present in the lecture.
IX The Hermit represents an individual assessment of results and separateness; this parallel is also stated in the lecture.
X The Wheel of Fortune represents a completed turn of the cycle and a transition to the next; this is why Tens are read as an outcome and a limit.
The lecture connects the Eight with karma, justice, and balance. This follows the older Marseille numbering, in which Justice is VIII. In Rider–Waite–Smith, Waite exchanged Strength and Justice: Strength became VIII and Justice XI. This article therefore preserves the author’s principle of Eight as cause and effect and balance, without presenting it as the numbering of the Waite deck itself.

What this approach offers in practice

Meanings cease to be a random set of keywords. You understand where an interpretation comes from.
An unfamiliar deck becomes easier to read: even if the artist has changed the details, number and suit still provide the basic structure.
Repeating patterns in a spread become easier to see: several Fives emphasize crisis; several Sixes, recovery and exchange; Nines, a personal outcome; Tens, completion.
Explaining cards to another person becomes easier. Instead of ‘this card means X,’ you can show the reasoning: ‘this number performs this function, and the suit places it in this area of life.’
The system reduces reliance on rote memorization while leaving room for the image, the context of the question, and intuitive reading.
